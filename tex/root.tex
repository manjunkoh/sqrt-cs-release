\begin{abstract}
We propose a novel method for solving the chance-constrained covariance steering problem. We explicitly write the propagation equations of the covariance matrix Cholesky factor using the QR decomposition. Due to this nonconvex representation, the problem is solved iteratively via sequential convex programming. Nevertheless, we prove that the local optimal solution obtained from this method corresponds to a local optimum when the covariance matrices are represented in its original form. Specifically when chance constraints are not present and the objective function is quadratic, the solution is globally optimal. The method also demonstrates good scalability for long horizon lengths, and numerical reliability for problems with small variance components compared to the full covariance approach.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Uncertainty is the fundamental reason for control design. Covariance steering is a control method that includes the mean and covariance in an optimal control problem, proving an optimal sequence of feedforward and feedback control policies that steers the moments to desired values. 

In many real-world applications, the uncertainty has magnitudes smaller than the mean values. This means that when solving chance-constrained covariance steering problems, in which the mean-only steering problem and the covariance-only steering problem are coupled, ill-scaling is inevitable. 

\subsection{Relevant Works}
Recent works on finite-horizon discrete-time covariance steering mainly focus on converting the problem into a convex optimization problem. Skaf and Boyd \cite{skafDesignAffineControllers2010} considers a state-history feedback controller and formulates the problem as a semidefinite programming (SDP) problem. Okamoto et al. \cite{okamotoOptimalCovarianceControl2018} later adds chance constraints while maintaining convexity. However, these methods suffer from the poor computational complexity as the horizon length increases, due to the large linear matrix inequality (LMI) constraints involved, as shown in \cite{rapakouliasDiscreteTimeOptimalCovariance2023}. Okamoto and Tsiotras \cite{okamotoOptimalStochasticVehicle2019} remedied the need for block lower triangular matrix variables employed in \cite{skafDesignAffineControllers2010,okamotoOptimalCovarianceControl2018} by introducing an alternative affine feedback control law, requiring only a block diagonal structure. Nevertheless, due to the implicit representation of the covariance matrix variables as a function of all the previous system matrices, the LMIs to be solved remain rather large. For later discussion, it is also important to note that in \cite{skafDesignAffineControllers2010,okamotoOptimalCovarianceControl2018,okamotoOptimalStochasticVehicle2019}, the terminal covariance and chance constraints are written as convex functions of the optimization variables by expressing the covariance matrix at each time step as a result of a propagation from the \textit{initial} covariance. As a result, the problem variables involve the square root of the covariance matrix variables. This allows affine chance constraints to be expressed exactly as second-order cone constraints. (See (42) and Proposition 4 of \cite{okamotoOptimalCovarianceControl2018} to observe that \((\cdot)^{1/2} \) appears in the deterministic formulation of the constraints.)

More recently, Liu et al. \cite{liuOptimalCovarianceSteering2025} made notable breakthroughs. It first shows the optimality of the affine feedback control law of form \cref{eq:feedback_control_law}. Second, it relaxes the nonconvex problem to an SDP, and shows that its first-order optimality conditions imply that the relaxation is tight. Compared to the block-matrix approaches (\cite{skafDesignAffineControllers2010,okamotoOptimalCovarianceControl2018,okamotoOptimalStochasticVehicle2019}), this method explicitly defines the covariance matrix variables and scales much better with the growth in problem size. Its proof of convexity has been extended to the chance-constrained problem \cite{rapakouliasDiscreteTimeOptimalCovariance2023}. 

At the same time, we have observed several drawbacks of \cite{liuOptimalCovarianceSteering2025} in application, which motivated this work: 
\begin{itemize}
\item (\textbf{Numerical Reliability}) In practical scenarios \cite{oguriChanceConstrainedControlSafe2024a,kumagaiRobustCislunarLowThrust2025a} where the state components have small variance compared to the mean values, this can lead to difficulties for numerical solvers. We hypothesize that the numerical difficulties can be attributed to dealing with the covariance matrix directly. This necessarily squares the system units and amplifies ill-scaling, causing issues for solvers working in finite precision. 

\item (\textbf{Non-convexity}) 
It is common to encounter objective functions or constraints that are represented as convex in the square root form, e.g. affine chance constraints. However, due to the square root operation, these constraints are nonconvex in the covariance matrix form.
Thus, a reference covariance matrix for conservatively approximating the square root via linearization \cite{rapakouliasDiscreteTimeOptimalCovariance2023} or iterative algorithms \cite{pilipovskyComputationallyEfficientChance2024} are required. 

\item (\textbf{Limitations of Convexification}) The proof of convexity in \cite{liuOptimalCovarianceSteering2025} requires that the partial of the the Lagrangian with respect to the control covariance matrix is positive definite (such as in the quadratic case). For non-quadratic objective functions, this is not always the case, so theoretical work is required to ensure the convexity of new formulations; for example, when using a non-quadratic objective function \cite{kumagaiRobustCislunarLowThrust2025a}, a small quadratic term was added to the objective function to ensure convexity.

\item (\textbf{Numerical Accuracy of Convexification}) The convexification process is only valid up to the numerical accuracy of the LMI that is solved. We have observed that when the solver terminates due to numerical issues, the covariance propagation equations can be lossy.
\end{itemize}

The approach we take, explicitly defining the covariance matrix Cholesky factor as an optimization variable and its propagation equation, is inspired by the square root Kalman filter. Along this line of literature, a notable work is by Thornton and Bierman \cite{thorntonNumericalComparisonDiscrete1976} which, in the 1970s, demonstrated the numerical instability of the standard Kalman filter compared to its equivalent counterparts that propagate factorized versions of the covariance matrix. These square root methods include the Cholesky \cite{dyerExtensionSquarerootFiltering1969} and LDL \cite{thorntonGramSchmidtAlgorithmsCovariance1975} factorizations. In fact, the square root filter was adopted for the Apollo Guidance Computer due to its numerical stability \cite{grewalApplicationsKalmanFiltering2010}. See \cite{kaminskiDiscreteSquareRoot1971,tapleyStatisticalOrbitDetermination2004} for a review and comparison of these methods.
We also note that this work is not the first to propagate the square root of the covariance matrix in the context of covariance steering. \cite{oguriStochasticSequentialConvex2022} addressed this with some shortcomings: the variable that represents the square root grows linearly in size with the horizon length, and the feedback gains are retrieved via a least-squares matrix inversion.

\subsection{Contributions}
We propose a novel method for solving the chance-constrained covariance steering problem. Due to the nonconvex representation of the covariance Cholesky factor propagation equations, the problem is solved iteratively via sequential convex programming. Nevertheless, the method is shown to be 1. globally optimal when the objective function is quadratic and chance constraints are not present, 2. more scalable for large horizon length than the block approach, and 3. numerically reliable for problems with small variance components compared to the full covariance approach. Furthermore, it is free of any proofs of convexification that rely on a specific objective function form (cf. \cite{liuOptimalCovarianceSteering2025}).

\section{Preliminaries}

\subsection{Notation}
$\mathbb{L}^n$ ($\mathbb{L}_+^n$) denotes the space of $n \times n$ lower triangular matrices (with positive diagonal entries). $\mathbb{S}^n_+$ denotes the space of $n \times n$ symmetric positive definite matrices. The Cholesky factor of a positive definite matrix \( P \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times n} \) is defined as the unique matrix \( S \in \mathbb{L}_+^n \) such that \( P = S S^\top \) \cite[Theorem 4.2.7]{golubMatrixComputations2013}; we write \(S = P^{1/2}\). $\mathrm{diag}(M)$ denotes the vector of diagonal elements of matrix \( M \), and $\mathrm{diag}(a,b, \cdots)$ denotes the diagonal matrix with $a,b, \cdots$ on the diagonals. The linear matrix inequality $A \succ (\succeq) B$ denotes that $A - B $ is positive (semi)definite. \(\N_{a}^{b}\) denotes the set of integers from \( a \) to \( b \).
We refer to the ``square root" of the covariance \(P \) to refer to (not necessarily square) matrices \(L\) that satisfy \(P = L L^\top\). 
$\mathrm{vectril}(\cdot)$ vectorizes the lower triangular part of a matrix.

\subsection{Problem Formulation}
We address the finite-horizon discrete-time covariance steering problem for a linear time-varying system with additive Gaussian noise. The goal is to find a state feedback controller that steers the state mean and covariance from their initial values to desired terminal values while minimizing a cost function and obeying chance constraints. Mathematically, the problem is formulated as follows.
\begin{subequations}
\label{eq:problem_statement}
\begin{align}
	&\minimize_{\bm{v}, \bm{K}} \quad J = \sum_{k=0}^{N-1} J_k (x_k, u_k) \\
	&\text{subject to} \\
	& x_{k+1} = A_k x_k + B_k u_k + G_k w_k, \quad k \in \N_{0}^{N-1} \\
	& u_k = v_k + K_k (x_k - \E{x_k}), \quad k \in \N_{0}^{N-1} \label{eq:feedback_control_law}\\
	& x_0 \sim \mathcal{N}(\mu_{\mathrm{init}}, P_{\mathrm{init}}) \\
	& x_N \sim \mathcal{N}(\mu_{\mathrm{fin}}, P_{\mathrm{fin}}) \\
	& P_{\mathrm{fin}} \succeq \E{(x_N - \E{x_N})(x_N - \E{x_N})^\top} \\
	& \mathbb{P}(x_k \in \mathcal{X}) \geq 1 - \epsilon_x, \quad k \in \N_{0}^{N} \label{eq:state_chance_constraint}\\ 
	& \mathbb{P}(u_k \in \mathcal{U}) \geq 1 - \epsilon_u, \quad k \in \N_{0}^{N-1} \label{eq:control_chance_constraint}
\end{align}
\end{subequations}
where \( x_k \in \mathbb{R}^n \) is the state vector, \( u_k \in \mathbb{R}^m \) is the control input, and \( w_k \in \mathbb{R}^n \) is standard Gaussian process noise. \( \bm{v} := \{v_k\}_{k=0}^{N-1}\) and \(\bm{K} := \{K_k\}_{k=0}^{N-1}\) are respectively the nominal control input and the state feedback gain to be designed, with \( v_k \in \mathbb{R}^m \) and \( K_k \in \mathbb{R}^{m \times n} \). We restrict the form of feedback control to \cref{eq:feedback_control_law} since it is shown to be optimal for the covariance steering problem without chance constraints \cite{liuOptimalCovarianceSteering2025} (i.e. \cref{eq:problem_statement} without \cref{eq:state_chance_constraint} and \cref{eq:control_chance_constraint}). Note that due to the affine feedback control law, the state and the control input are Gaussian at each time step.
The matrices \( A_k \), \( B_k \), and \( G_k \) are of appropriate dimensions, with the following assumption on \(G_k\):
\begin{assumption} \label{assump:full_rank_G}
	\( \mathrm{rank}(G_k) = n \) for all \( k \in \N_{0}^{N-1} \).
\end{assumption}
This assumption is reasonable as the process noise is usually discretized from a continuous-time process. 

We consider two types of cost functions:
\begin{equation} \label{eq:objective_function}
	J_k (x_k, u_k) = \begin{cases}
	\E{x_k^\top Q_k x_k + u_k^\top R_k u_k} & \text{(quadratic)} \\
	\mathcal{Q}_{\norm{u_k}_2}(p_J) & \text{(quantile 2-norm)}
	\end{cases}
\end{equation}
with \( Q_k \succeq 0 \) and \( R_k \succ 0 \), and \( \mathcal{Q}_{\norm{u_k}_2}(p_J) \) is $p_J$-quantile of the 2-norm of the control input $u_k$. The latter cost is used in scenarios where the probabilistically worst-case control effort is of more interest than the expected value, such as in spacecraft mission design \cite{oguriChanceConstrainedControlSafe2024a}.

The sets \( \mathcal{X} \) and \( \mathcal{U} \) represent state and control feasible sets, respectively, with associated violation probabilities \( \epsilon_x \) and \( \epsilon_u \). Through some risk allocation procedure between each node, we assume that the chance constraints are given in one of two forms: a linear constraint or a norm constraint. For the linear constraint form, the chance constraint is given by:
\begin{equation} \label{eq:linear_chance_constraint}
	\mathbb{P}(\alpha_j ^\top x_k \leq \beta_j) \geq 1 - p_j
\end{equation}
for each linear constraint defined by \( \alpha_j \in \mathbb{R}^n \) and \( \beta_j \in \mathbb{R} \), with violation probability \( p_j \).
For the norm constraint form, the chance constraint is given by:
\begin{equation} \label{eq:norm_chance_constraint}
	\mathbb{P}(\| x_k \|_2 \leq \gamma) \geq 1 - p_\gamma
\end{equation}
for some scalar \( \gamma > 0 \) and violation probability \( p_\gamma \). The same forms apply to the control input \( u_k \).

For convenience, we make the following assumption, which is reasonable in practical applications:
\begin{assumption} \label{assump:positive_definite_state}
	The state covariance remains strictly positive definite throughout the horizon.
\end{assumption}


\subsection{QR Decomposition}

The QR decomposition is a key element of propagating the square root of the covariance matrix, \textit{while keeping the covariance square root to be in \(\mathbb{L}^n\)}. Various algorithms have been proposed for the time update of the square root Kalman filter, but many can be expressed as a QR decomposition \cite{tapleyStatisticalOrbitDetermination2004}.

\begin{lemma}[Existence and uniqueness of economy-size QR decomposition, \cite{ipsenNumericalMatrixAnalysis2009}]\label{lemma:existence_and_uniqueness_of_economy_size_qr_decomposition}
	Let \( M \in \mathbb{R}^{p \times q} \), \(p \geq q\).
	\begin{itemize}
		\item (Existence) There exist \(Q \in \mathbb{R}^{p \times q}\) and \(R \in \mathbb{R}^{q \times q}\) such that \(Q Q^\top = I\), \(R\) is upper triangular, and \(M = QR\).
		\item (Uniqueness) If \(M\) has full rank, i.e. \(\mathrm{rank}(M) = q\), then the matrices \(Q\) and \(R\) are unique up to a sign change of the diagonal entries of \(R\). If we require the diagonal entries of \( R \) to be positive, then the decomposition is unique.
	\end{itemize}
\end{lemma}
From here on, we will use the phrase ``QR decomposition'' to refer to the economy-size QR decomposition with positive diagonal entries.
\footnote{In MATLAB, this requires using $\mathrm{qr}(M, \text{``econ"})$. Then, to ensure uniqueness, if the function returns an \( R \) matrix with negative diagonal entries, multiply the corresponding columns of \( Q \) and rows of \( R \) by -1 to ensure positive diagonals.}

\begin{remark}
	If \(M\) is full rank, then so is \(R\), hence \(R\) is invertible.
\end{remark}

\subsection{QR Decomposition Derivative}
In order to use this square root covariance propagation in an optimization framework, we utilize the derivative of the QR decomposition operation (i.e. the sensitivity of the $Q$ and $R$ matrices with respect to perturbations in the $M$ matrix). $\dd R$ is written as a function of \(Q\), \( R\), and  $\dd M$ \cite{deleeuwDifferentiatingQRDecomposition2023}:
\begin{equation}
	\dd R = \dd R(\dd M, Q, R) = Q^\top (\dd M) - \mathrm{tril}(Q^\top (\dd M) R^{-1}) R.
\end{equation}
Using the Taylor expansion, we can approximate the QR decomposition as a linear function of perturbations in \( M \), around some reference \( \bar{M} = \bar{Q} \bar{R} \):
\begin{equation}
	\mathrm{qr}(M) \approx \bar{R} + \dd R(M - \bar{M}, \bar{Q}, \bar{R}).
\end{equation}
where \( \mathrm{qr}(\cdot) \) denotes the operation of extracting the upper triangular factor \( R \) from the QR decomposition.
\( \dd Q \) can also be computed as a function of \( \dd M\) \cite{deleeuwDifferentiatingQRDecomposition2023}; it is not used for this work, so we omit it here.

\section{Deterministic Formulation via Square Root Covariance Propagation}
By applying the previous results, we can formulate \cref{eq:problem_statement} as a nonconvex deterministic optimization problem.
\subsection{Square Root Covariance Propagation with Feedback Control}
Since the state is controlled by the affine feedback control law, the closed-loop dynamics are given for all $k \in \N_{0}^{N-1}$ as
\begin{equation}
	x_{k+1} = A_k x_k + B_k (v_k + K_k (x_k - \E{x_k})) + G_k w_k.
\end{equation}
Taking the expectation, the mean dynamics are given by
\begin{equation} \label{eq:mean_dynamics}
	\mu_{k+1} = A_k \mu_k + B_k v_k
\end{equation}
where $\mu_k := \E{x_k}$.
By defining 
\begin{equation}
	P_k := \E{(x_k - \E{x_k})(x_k - \E{x_k})^\top},
\end{equation}
the covariance propagation equation is given by:
\begin{equation} \label{eq:full_covariance_propagation}
	P_{k+1} = (A_k + B_k K_k) P_k (A_k + B_k K_k)^T + G_k G_k^\top.
\end{equation}

\begin{lemma}[Equivalent Square Root Covariance Propagation]
	The covariance propagation equation \cref{eq:full_covariance_propagation} is equivalently expressed in its Cholesky factor form using $S_k = P_k^{1/2} \in \mathbb{L}_+^n$ as:
	\begin{equation} \label{eq:square_root_covariance_propagation_nonlinear_feedback_control}
		S_{k+1} = (\mathrm{qr}(X_{k+1}^\top))^\top, \quad
		X_{k+1} := \begin{bmatrix} (A_k + B_k K_k) S_k & G_k \end{bmatrix}.
	\end{equation}
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
If we define \( S_k = P_k^{1/2} \), the covariance propagation can be expressed as:
\begin{equation} \label{eq:covariance_propagation}
	S_{k+1} S_{k+1}^T = X_{k+1} X_{k+1}^T
\end{equation}
with \(X_{k+1}\) defined above.
Note that we want $S_{k+1} \in \mathbb{L}_+^n$, while $X_{k+1} \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times (n+m)}$.
For a matrix $Q_{k} \in \R^{(n+m) \times n}$ such that $Q_{k} Q_{k}^\top = I$,
\begin{equation} \label{eq:Q_S_X_relation}
\begin{split}
     X_{k+1}^\top &= Q_{k} S_{k+1}^\top \\
	\implies X_{k+1} X_{k+1}^\top &= S_{k+1} Q_{k}^\top Q_{k} S_{k+1}^\top = S_{k+1} S_{k+1}^\top.
\end{split}
\end{equation}
Since $S_{k+1} \in \mathbb{L}_+^n$, its transpose is upper triangular, so the first equality in \cref{eq:Q_S_X_relation} is a QR decomposition of $X_{k+1}^\top$. By \cref{lemma:existence_and_uniqueness_of_economy_size_qr_decomposition}, if $X_{k+1}^\top$ is full rank, there exists a unique set of matrices $(Q_{k}, S_{k+1})$ that satisfy the equation.
% In other words, the square root covariance propagation can be expressed as a nonlinear operation:
% \begin{equation} \label{eq:square_root_covariance_propagation_nonlinear_feedback_control}
% 	S_{k+1} = (\mathrm{qr}(X_{k+1}^\top))^\top
% \end{equation}
 Since the column space of \(X_{k+1}\) includes the column space of \(G_k\), and since $G_k$ is full rank by \cref{assump:full_rank_G}, it follows that \(X_{k+1}\) is full rank. Then, it follows that this QR decomposition is unique. 
\end{proof}
In other words, $S_{k+1}$ obtained from \cref{eq:square_root_covariance_propagation_nonlinear_feedback_control} provides a unique matrix in $\mathbb{L}_n^+$ such that \cref{eq:full_covariance_propagation} is satisfied.

Since both $P_k$ and $K_k$ in \cref{eq:square_root_covariance_propagation_nonlinear_feedback_control} are unknowns, there is a bilinear term \( K_k S_k \) in \cref{eq:square_root_covariance_propagation_nonlinear_feedback_control}; for the sake of convexity, we perform a change of variables and define $L_k = K_k S_k \in \mathbb{R}^{m \times n}$. Then, $K_k$ can be uniquely recovered as $K_k = L_k S_k^{-1}$. The inverse exists since $S_k$ is the Cholesky factor of \( P_k \), which is positive definite by \cref{assump:positive_definite_state}.
So we get
\begin{equation} \label{eq:square_root_covariance_propagation}
	S_{k+1} = (\mathrm{qr}(X_{k+1}^\top))^\top, \quad X_{k+1} = \begin{bmatrix} A_k S_k + B_k L_k & G_k \end{bmatrix}
\end{equation}
with its first-order approximation given by:
\begin{equation} \label{eq:square_root_covariance_propagation_linearized}
	S_{k+1} \approx (\mathrm{qr}(\bar{X}_{k+1}^\top))^\top + \left( \dd R(X_{k+1}^\top - \bar{X}_{k+1}^\top, \bar{Q}_{k+1}, \bar{R}_{k+1}) \right)^\top
\end{equation}
where \( \bar{X}_{k+1} = \begin{bmatrix} A_k \bar{S}_k + B_k \bar{L}_k & G_k \end{bmatrix} \), \(\bar{S}_k\) and \(\bar{L}_k\) being the reference values, and \(\bar{Q}_{k+1} \bar{R}_{k+1} = \bar{X}_{k+1}\) being the QR decomposition of \(\bar{X}_{k+1}\). 

\subsection{Objective Function}
After substituting \( u_k = K_k x_k = L_k S_k^{-1} x_k \), and performing some standard manipulations, the objective function can be expressed as deterministic convex functions of \( L_k \) and \( S_k \):
\begin{equation} \label{eq:objective_deterministic}
	J = \begin{cases}
	\sum_{k=0}^{N-1} \mu_k^\top Q_k \mu_k + v_k^\top R_k v_k & \\
	\quad + \mathrm{trace} (Q_k S_k S_k^\top + R_k L_k L_k^\top) & \text{(quadratic)} \\
	\sum_{k=0}^{N-1} \norm{v_k}_2 + \sqrt{\mathcal{Q}_{\chi^2_m}(p_J)} \ \norm{L_k}_2 & \text{(quantile 2-norm)}.
	\end{cases}
\end{equation}
The 2-norm cost is a conservative approximation \cite{oguriChanceConstrainedControlSafe2024a}; \( \mathcal{Q}_{\chi^2_m}(\cdot) \) is the inverse of the chi-squared cumulative distribution function with \( m \) degrees of freedom.
\subsection{Constraints}
Thanks to the square root representation, we can also rewrite the constraints as convex functions of the variables.

The structure of \(S_k\) is imposed by
\begin{equation} \label{eq:S_structure_constraint}
	S_k \in \mathbb{L}^n, \quad \mathrm{diag}(S_k) \geq 0, \quad k \in \N_{0}^{N}.
\end{equation}
The initial distributional constraint is imposed by
\begin{equation} \label{eq:initial_distributional_constraint}
	\mu_0 = \mu_{\mathrm{init}}, \quad S_0 = P_{\mathrm{init}}^{1/2}.
\end{equation}
The terminal distributional constraint is imposed by \cite{okamotoOptimalCovarianceControl2018}
\begin{equation} \label{eq:terminal_distributional_constraint}
	\mu_N = \mu_{\mathrm{fin}}, \quad \norm{P_{\mathrm{fin}}^{-1/2} S_N}_2 - 1 \leq 0
\end{equation}
where \( \norm{\cdot}_2 \) denotes the spectral norm here.

Since we are working with the square root form, chance constraints can be imposed in a convex manner.
For the linear constraint form \cref{eq:linear_chance_constraint}, the chance constraint can be exactly expressed as \cite{okamotoOptimalCovarianceControl2018}:
\begin{equation} \label{eq:linear_chance_constraint_deterministic}
	\alpha_j^\top \mu_k + \mathcal{Q}_{\mathcal{N}}(1 - p_j) \norm{S_k^\top \alpha_j}_2 - \beta_j \leq 0, \quad k \in \N_{0}^{N}
\end{equation}
where \( \mathcal{Q}_{\mathcal{N}}(p_j) \) is the inverse of the standard normal cumulative distribution function.
For the norm constraint form \cref{eq:norm_chance_constraint}, similar to the quantile 2-norm cost, the chance constraint can be conservatively imposed as \cite{oguriChanceConstrainedControlSafe2024a}:
\begin{equation} \label{eq:norm_chance_constraint_deterministic}
	\norm{\mu_k}_2 + \sqrt{Q_{\chi^2_n}(1 - p_\gamma)} \ \norm{S_k}_2 - \gamma \leq 0, \quad k \in \N_{0}^{N}
\end{equation}
The control chance constraints can be imposed by replacing \( \mu_k \) with \( v_k \) and \( S_k \) with \( L_k \).

To summarize, \cref{eq:problem_statement} is converted into a nonconvex optimization problem as follows:
\begin{equation} \label{eq:nonconvex_problem_statement}
	\minimize_{\bm{v}, \bm{L}, \bm{\mu}, \bm{S}} \cref{eq:objective_deterministic} \quad \text{s.t.} 
	\quad 
	\text{\cref{eq:S_structure_constraint,eq:mean_dynamics,eq:square_root_covariance_propagation,eq:initial_distributional_constraint,eq:terminal_distributional_constraint,eq:linear_chance_constraint_deterministic,eq:norm_chance_constraint_deterministic,eq:trust_region_constraint}}
\end{equation}
where the only source of nonconvexity is the square root covariance dynamics \cref{eq:square_root_covariance_propagation}. For notational simplicity, we define \(\bm{L} := \{L_k\}_{k=0}^{N-1}\), \(\bm{\mu} := \{\mu_k\}_{k=0}^{N}\), and \(\bm{S} := \{S_k\}_{k=0}^{N}\).

\section{Algorithm Overview}
In order to take advantage of the convex representation of the objective and constraints, we use a specific sequential convex programming (SCP) algorithm \texttt{SCvx*} \cite{oguriSuccessiveConvexificationFeasibility2023a} to solve \cref{eq:nonconvex_problem_statement}; this ensures that the nonlinear dynamics are obeyed at convergence, and the converged solution is locally optimal. At each iteration, the linearized dynamics \cref{eq:square_root_covariance_propagation_linearized} are imposed with penalization of violations:
\begin{equation} \label{eq:linearized_dynamics}
\begin{split}
	&\mathrm{vectril} \left\{ S_{k+1} - (\mathrm{qr}(\bar{X}_{k+1}^\top))^\top \right. \\
	&\left. - \left( \dd R(X_{k+1}^\top - \bar{X}_{k+1}^\top, \bar{Q}_{k+1}, \bar{R}_{k+1}) \right)^\top \right\} = \xi_k
\end{split}
\end{equation}
where \( \xi_k \in \mathbb{R}^{\frac{n(n+1)}{2}}\) is a slack variable, penalized in the objective function via an Augmented Lagrangian method in the \texttt{SCvx*} algorithm. 

The reference QR matrices $\{\bar{Q}_{k+1}, \bar{R}_{k+1}\}$ are computed at initialization and updated whenever a step is accepted:
\begin{equation} \label{eq:reference_update}
	\{\bar{Q}_{k+1}, \bar{R}_{k+1}\} \gets \mathrm{qr}(\begin{bmatrix} A_k \bar{S}_k + B_k \bar{L}_k & G_k \end{bmatrix}),  k \in \N_{0}^{N-1}
\end{equation}

To ensure that the linearization process does not cause artificial infeasibility/unboundedness \cite{maoSuccessiveConvexificationNonconvex2016}, we impose a trust region constraint:
\begin{align} \label{eq:trust_region_constraint}
	\norm{\mathrm{vectril}(D_S(S_k - \bar{S}_k))}_\infty &\leq r, \quad k \in \N_{1}^{N-1}, \\
	\norm{\mathrm{vec}(D_L(L_k - \bar{L}_k))}_\infty &\leq r, \quad k \in \N_{0}^{N-1}
\end{align}
where \( r > 0 \) is the trust region radius, and \( D_S \) and \( D_L \) are diagonal matrices for scaling.
Trust regions on \( \mu_k \) and \( v_k \) are not necessary, since they only appear in the convex constraints.


The objective function is augmented with a penalty term using the augmented Lagrangian method. The penalty function for the constraint violations is defined as:
\begin{equation} \label{eq:penalty_function}
	P(\bm{\xi}; w, \bm{\lambda}) = \sum_{k=0}^{N-1} \left( \lambda_k^\top \xi_k + \frac{w}{2} \|\xi_k\|_2^2 \right)
\end{equation}
where \( \lambda_k \in \mathbb{R}^{\frac{n(n+1)}{2}} \) are Lagrange multipliers, \( w > 0\) is the penalty weight, and \(\bm{\xi} := \{\xi_k\}_{k=0}^{N-1}\) with \(\xi_k \in \mathbb{R}^{\frac{n(n+1)}{2}}\) are the slack variables from \cref{eq:linearized_dynamics}. For convenience, define \(\bm{z} := \{\bm{v}, \bm{L}, \bm{\mu}, \bm{S} \} \). The augmented objective function is then:
\begin{equation} \label{eq:objective_augmented}
	\mathcal{L}(\bm{z}, \bm{\xi}; w, \bm{\lambda}) := 
	J(\bm{z}) + P(\bm{\xi}; w, \bm{\lambda})
\end{equation}
where \( \bm{\lambda} := \{\lambda_k\}_{k=0}^{N-1} \). In the \texttt{SCvx*} algorithm, the Lagrange multipliers and penalty weights are updated throughout the iterations to ensure convergence to a feasible and locally optimal solution.

The subproblem is then given by:
\begin{equation} \label{eq:subproblem}
	\minimize_{\bm{v}, \bm{L}, \bm{\mu}, \bm{S}, \bm{\xi}} \cref{eq:objective_augmented} \quad \text{s.t.} \quad 
	\text{\cref{eq:S_structure_constraint,eq:mean_dynamics,eq:linearized_dynamics,eq:initial_distributional_constraint,eq:terminal_distributional_constraint,eq:linear_chance_constraint_deterministic,eq:norm_chance_constraint_deterministic,eq:trust_region_constraint}}.
\end{equation}

The complete solution process is summarized in Algorithm \ref{alg:sqrt_qr_covariance_steering}. At each iteration, the algorithm solves the convex subproblem \cref{eq:subproblem} to obtain a candidate solution. 
The algorithm then evaluates the following quantities to determine step acceptance and parameter updates:
\begin{align}
	\widetilde{\bm{\xi}}(\bm{z}) & := \begin{bmatrix}
		\mathrm{vectril}(S_1 - (\mathrm{qr}(X_1^{\top}))^\top) \\
		\vdots \\
		\mathrm{vectril}(S_N - (\mathrm{qr}(X_N^{\top}))^\top)
	\end{bmatrix} \\
	\mathcal{J}(\bm{z}, w, \bm{\lambda}) &:= J(\bm{z}) + P({\widetilde{\bm{\xi}}}(\bm{z}); w, \bm{\lambda}) \\
	\Delta J^{(i)} &:= \mathcal{J}(\bar{\bm{z}}; w^{(i)}, \bm{\lambda}^{(i)}) - \mathcal{J}(\bm{z}^*; w^{(i)}, \bm{\lambda}^{(i)}) \label{eq:delta_J} \\
	\Delta L^{(i)} &:= \mathcal{J}(\bar{\bm{z}}; w^{(i)}, \bm{\lambda}^{(i)}) - \mathcal{L}(\bm{z}^*, \bm{\xi}^*; w^{(i)}, \bm{\lambda}^{(i)}) \label{eq:delta_L} \\
	\chi^{(i)} &:= \norm{\widetilde{\bm{\xi}}(\bm{z})}_2 \label{eq:chi}
\end{align}
where the superscript $(i)$ denotes the SCP iteration number, and $^*$ denotes the solution obtained at the current iteration. Here, $\widetilde{\bm{\xi}}$ is the vector of infeasibility of the nonlinear square root covariance dynamics, $\mathcal{J}$ is the augmented cost penalizing the nonlinear infeasibility,  $\Delta J^{(i)}$ is the actual cost reduction, $\Delta L^{(i)}$ is the predicted cost reduction from the linearized subproblem, and $\chi^{(i)}$ measures the norm of nonlinear infeasibility. The step acceptance ratio $\rho^{(i)} = \Delta J^{(i)} / \Delta L^{(i)}$ is computed, where $\Delta L^{(i)} \geq 0$ is guaranteed by the convexity of the subproblem \cite{oguriSuccessiveConvexificationFeasibility2023a}. If $\Delta L = 0$, one can set $\rho^{(i)} = 1$. The step is accepted if $\rho^{(i)} \geq \rho_0$ for some $\rho_0 \in (0,1)$.

When the step is accepted and $|\Delta J^{(i)}| < \delta^{(i)}$ for a stationarity tolerance $\delta^{(i)} > 0$, the Lagrange multipliers and stationarity tolerance are updated. The trust region radius and penalty weight are updated based on the step acceptance ratio, where $\alpha_1 > 1$, $\alpha_2 > 1$, $\beta > 1$, and $0 < \rho_1 < \rho_0 < \rho_2 < 1$ are algorithm parameters.

The algorithm terminates when both $|\Delta J^{(i)}| \leq \epsilon_{\mathrm{opt}}$ and $\chi^{(i)} \leq \epsilon_{\mathrm{feas}}$ are satisfied, where $\epsilon_{\mathrm{opt}} > 0$ and $\epsilon_{\mathrm{feas}} > 0$ are user-specified tolerances. Ref. \cite{oguriSuccessiveConvexificationFeasibility2023a} provides more details of the $\texttt{SCvx*}$ algorithm, including its proof of convergence to a local minimum.

\begin{algorithm}[thbp]
\caption{Square Root QR Covariance Steering via \texttt{SCvx*}}
\label{alg:sqrt_qr_covariance_steering}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\Require Initial guess $\bar{\bm{L}}, \bar{\bm{S}}$
\Require SCP parameters: $\epsilon_{\mathrm{feas}}$, $\epsilon_{\mathrm{opt}}$, $\rho_1$, $\rho_0$, $\rho_2$, $\alpha_1$, $\alpha_2$, $\beta$, $\gamma$, $r_{\mathrm{min}}$, $r_{\mathrm{max}}$, $r_{\mathrm{init}}$, $w_{\mathrm{init}}$
\State Initialize: $i \gets 1$, $r^{(1)} \gets r_{\mathrm{init}}$, $w^{(1)} \gets w_{\mathrm{init}}$, $\delta^{(1)} \gets \infty$, $\bm{\lambda}^{(1)} \gets \bm{0}$
\State Compute reference QR matrices using \cref{eq:reference_update}
\While{$|\Delta J^{(i)}| > \epsilon_{\mathrm{opt}}$ or $\chi^{(i)} > \epsilon_{\mathrm{feas}}$}
	\State Solve convex subproblem: $(\bm{v}^*, \bm{L}^*, \bm{\mu}^*, \bm{S}^*, \bm{\xi}^*) \gets \arg\min$ \cref{eq:subproblem}
	\State Compute $\Delta J^{(i)}$, $\Delta L^{(i)}$, and $\chi^{(i)}$ using \cref{eq:delta_J,eq:delta_L,eq:chi}
	\State Compute step acceptance ratio: $\rho^{(i)} \gets \Delta J^{(i)} / \Delta L^{(i)}$
	\If{$\rho^{(i)} \geq \rho_0$} \Comment{Accept step}
		\State $(\bar{\bm{v}}, \bar{\bm{L}}, \bar{\bm{\mu}}, \bar{\bm{S}}) \gets (\bm{v}^*, \bm{L}^*, \bm{\mu}^*, \bm{S}^*)$
		\State Update reference QR matrices using \cref{eq:reference_update}
		\If{$|\Delta J^{(i)}| < \delta^{(i)}$}
			\State $\lambda_k^{(i+1)} \gets \lambda_k^{(i)} + w^{(i)} \xi_k^*$ for $k \in \N_{0}^{N-1}$
			\State $\delta^{(i+1)} \gets \begin{cases}
				|\Delta J^{(i)}| & \text{if } \delta^{(i)} = \infty \\
				\gamma \delta^{(i)} & \text{otherwise}
			\end{cases}$
			\State $w^{(i+1)} \gets \beta w^{(i)}$
		\Else
			\State $\bm{\lambda}^{(i+1)} \gets \bm{\lambda}^{(i)}$, $\delta^{(i+1)} \gets \delta^{(i)}$, $w^{(i+1)} \gets w^{(i)}$
		\EndIf
	\Else
		\State $\bm{\lambda}^{(i+1)} \gets \bm{\lambda}^{(i)}$, $\delta^{(i+1)} \gets \delta^{(i)}$, $w^{(i+1)} \gets w^{(i)}$
	\EndIf
	\State Update trust region radius: 
	\State \quad $r^{(i+1)} \gets \begin{cases} \max(r^{(i)} / \alpha_1, r_{\mathrm{min}}) & \text{if } \rho^{(i)} < \rho_1 \\ r^{(i)} & \text{elseif } \rho^{(i)} < \rho_2 \\  \min(\alpha_2 r^{(i)}, r_{\mathrm{max}}) & \text{otherwise} \end{cases}$
	\State $i \gets i + 1$
\EndWhile
\State Recover feedback gains: $K_k^* \gets L_k^* (S_k^*)^{-1}$ for $k \in \N_{0}^{N-1}$
\State \Return $\bm{v}^*, \bm{K}^*, \bm{\mu}^*, \bm{S}^*$
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\section{Local and Global Optimality of the Converged Solution}

In this section, we connect the optimality of the solution obtained from \cref{alg:sqrt_qr_covariance_steering} (in the $\mathbb{L}_+^n$ coordinates) to that obtained from solving it defining the covariance matrix as variables, as in \cite{liuOptimalCovarianceSteering2025} (in the $\mathbb{S}_+^n$ coordinates).

 Consider two constrained optimization problems on manifolds $\mathcal{M}_A$ and $\mathcal{M}_B$ with variables $x \in \mathcal{M}_A$ and $y \in \mathcal{M}_B$:%
\begin{align*}
\text{(A)} \quad & \minimize_{x \in \mathcal{X}} \ f(x)\\
\text{(B)} \quad & \minimize_{y \in \mathcal{Y}} \ f(T(y))
\end{align*}
where $T: \mathcal{M}_B \rightarrow \mathcal{M}_A$ is a diffeomorphism such that $T(\mathcal{M}_B) = \mathcal{M}_A$, with equality constraints $g_i, i \in \mathcal{I}$ and inequality constraints $h_j, j \in \mathcal{J}$:
\begin{align}
	\mathcal{X} &:= \{ x \in \mathcal{M}_A \ |\  g_i(x) = 0, i \in \mathcal{I}; \ h_j(x) \leq 0 \in \mathcal{J} \} \\ 
	\mathcal{Y} &:= \{ y \in \mathcal{M}_B \ |\  g_i(T(y)) = 0, i \in \mathcal{I}; \ h_j(T(y)) \leq 0\in \mathcal{J} \} 
\end{align}
From the definition, $\mathcal{X} = T(\mathcal{Y})$.

\begin{proposition}[Invariance of Local Optimality via a Diffeomorphism]
\label{prop:global_optimality_diffeomorphism}
Let $y^*$ be a local minimum of (B). Then $x^* = T(y^*)$ is a local minimum of (A).
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
Since $y^*$ is a local minimum of (B), there exists a neighborhood $\mathcal{N}$ around $y^*$ such that
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:local_minimum}
f(T(y)) \geq f(T(y^*)) \quad \forall y \in \mathcal{S}_B
\end{equation}
where $\mathcal{S}_B := N \cap \mathcal{Y}$ \cite[Chapter 12]{nocedalNumericalOptimization2006}.

Since $T$ is a diffeomorphism, it is in particular a homeomorphism. Therefore, $T$ maps the open neighborhood $\mathcal{N}$ to an open set $T(\mathcal{N})$ which contains $T(y^*) = x^*$ \cite[Chapter 12]{munkresTopology2014}.
Defining $\mathcal{S}_A := T(N) \cap \mathcal{X}$,
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{S}_A = T(\mathcal{N}) \cap T(\mathcal{Y}) 
= T(\mathcal{N} \cap \mathcal{Y}) 
= T(\mathcal{S}_B)
\end{equation}
where the second equality follows from the fact that $T$ is in particular a bijection.
Therefore, for any $x \in \mathcal{S}_A$, there exists a unique pre-image $y = T^{-1}(x) \in \mathcal{S}_B$. From \cref{eq:local_minimum}, we have:
\begin{align}
\quad f(T(y)) &\geq f(T(y^*)) \quad \forall y \in \mathcal{S}_B \nonumber \\
\Rightarrow \quad f(x) &\geq f(x^*) \quad \forall x \in \mathcal{S}_A. \label{eq:local_optimality_x}
\end{align}
Hence, \(x^*\) is a local minimum of (A).
\end{proof}

Next, we apply this result to our specific problem. The following proposition is used in the proof.
\begin{proposition}[\cite{linRiemannianGeometrySymmetric2019}]
	\label{prop:cholesky_diffeomorphism}
	The Cholesky map \(\mathcal{L} : \mathbb{S}^n_+ \rightarrow \mathbb{L}_+^n\) is a diffeomorphism.
\end{proposition}

\begin{theorem} \label{th:local_optimality}
	\cref{alg:sqrt_qr_covariance_steering} converges to a locally optimal solution of the chance-constrained covariance steering problem, and its local optimality is invariant to whether the solution method uses the full covariance matrix. 
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
	In this specific scenario, (A) from \cref{prop:global_optimality_diffeomorphism} is obtained by the method in \cite{liuOptimalCovarianceSteering2025} with variable $x \in \mathcal{M}_A = \mathbb{S}_+^n \times \cdots \times \mathbb{S}_+^n \times \R^{n\times m} \times \cdots \times \R^{n \times m}$ where the first $N+1$ spaces are the positive definite matrices $P_k$ and the next $N$ spaces are the feedback gains $K_k$. (B) from \cref{prop:global_optimality_diffeomorphism} is \cref{eq:nonconvex_problem_statement} before the change of variables $L_k = K_k S_k$, with variable $y \in \mathcal{M}_B = 
	\mathbb{L}_+^n \times \cdots \times \mathbb{L}_+^n \times \R^{n\times m} \times \cdots \times \R^{n \times m}$, where the first $N+1$ spaces are $S_k$ and the next $N$ spaces are the feedback gains $K_k$. (We can ignore the mean variables $\bm{v}, \bm{\mu}$ as they appear exactly in the same way in either problem.)
	(B) is solved via \cref{alg:sqrt_qr_covariance_steering}, and the diffeomorphism $T: \mathcal{M}_B \rightarrow \mathcal{M}_A$ maps each of the matrices $\mathbb{S}_+^n$ to $\mathbb{L}_+^n$ via the inverse of the Cholesky map \(\mathcal{L} : \mathbb{S}^n_+ \rightarrow \mathbb{L}_+^n\) and does not modify the feedback gains $K_k$. The inverse of a diffeomorphism is also a diffeomorphism.
	Then, the proof follows from the local optimality of the \texttt{SCvx*} algorithm \cite[Theorem 3]{oguriSuccessiveConvexificationFeasibility2023a}. 
\end{proof}

\begin{theorem} \label{th:global_optimality}
	When the chance constraints are not present and the objective takes the quadratic form of \cref{eq:objective_function}, the solution is globally optimal.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
	The proof follows from the fact that the unconstrained covariance steering problem with quadratic cost is shown to be a convex optimization problem \cite{liuOptimalCovarianceSteering2025}. Since a local optimum of a convex problem is also a global optimum, from \cref{th:local_optimality}, the locally optimal solution obtained from \cref{alg:sqrt_qr_covariance_steering} is also globally optimal.
\end{proof}

\subsection{Optimality and Scalability}
Here, we perform a horizon-length scalability analysis, and also compare the solution costs between Liu et al. \cite{liuOptimalCovarianceSteering2025}, Okamoto and Tsiotras \cite{okamotoOptimalStochasticVehicle2019}, and the proposed method. We consider a three-dimensional double integrator system with a total horizon time of \(T = 3\) seconds, and vary the horizon length from 10 to 80 (so the step size is \(\Delta t = T / N\)). 
The time-invariant dynamics are given by
\begin{equation}
	A = \begin{bmatrix}
		I_3 & \Delta t I_3 \\
		0_{3 \times 3} & I_3
	\end{bmatrix},
	B = \begin{bmatrix}
		0.5 \Delta t^2 I_3 \\
		\Delta t I_3
	\end{bmatrix},
	G = \sqrt{q \Delta t} I_6
\end{equation}
with \( q = 0.05 \) being the process noise spectral density.
The initial and terminal state covariance are \(P_{\mathrm{init}} = I_6\) and \(P_{\mathrm{final}} = 0.5 P_{\mathrm{init}}\), respectively. We use a quadratic cost function, with \(Q = 0.1 I_6\) and \(R = I_3\).
The results are shown in \cref{fig:horizon_size_scalability,fig:horizon_size_cost_comparision}. To time the solutions, we perform 5 trials for each method for each horizon length. Convergence and feasibility tolerance for our method are set to \( \epsilon_{\mathrm{opt}} = \epsilon_{\mathrm{feas}} = 10^{-4}\). The scaling matrices \(D_S\) and \(D_L\) are set to identity matrices.

For this unconstrained problem, \cite{liuOptimalCovarianceSteering2025} offers fast and optimal solutions. \cite{okamotoOptimalStochasticVehicle2019} shows much worse scalability, which matches the observations in \cite{rapakouliasDiscreteTimeOptimalCovariance2023}. Another observation is that due to its specific feedback policy, it compromises optimality. Our approach shows scalability that is between the two, while achieving optimality, which empirically validates \cref{th:global_optimality}.

The results are shown in \cref{fig:example_cwh_trajectory,fig:example_cwh_control_history}, along with the results of 1000 Monte Carlo simulations. We can observe that the terminal covariance constrains is satisfied with equality, and the Monte Carlo simulations are within the 3-sigma bounds.

For the full covariance approach, since it cannot directly handle the ``square-root of maximum eigenvalue'' term, we linearize it around a reference covariance matrix, as in the chance constraint handling described in \cite{rapakouliasDiscreteTimeOptimalCovariance2023}. In this case, a small term \(\epsilon \trace(Y_k)\) is added to the objective function to ensure valid convexification \cite{kumagaiRobustCislunarLowThrust2025a}, with \(\epsilon = 10^{-3}\) chosen. The full covariance approach is not able to solve the problem, as MOSEK returns status \texttt{UNKNOWN}. This indicates numerical issues, and the returned solution may or may not be close to a feasible and optimal solution \cite{mosekapsMOSEKOptimizationToolbox2025}. For this problem, we checked that the covariance dynamics are largely violated. This supports our hypothesis: by working with the covariance matrix which necessarily squares the units of deviation, numerical solvers are more likely to encounter problems. We also tested with alternative objective functions, such as the quadratic cost function, \( \sum_k \norm{v_k}_2 + \lambda_{\max}(Y_k) + \epsilon \trace(Y_k)\), and \(\sum_k \norm{v_k}_2 + \trace(Y_k) \), which either resulted in numerical issues and/or resulted in lossy covariance propagation. We measured the loss in covariance propagation as follows:
\begin{equation}
	\text{Loss} = \norm{\phi(K_k, P_k) - P_{k+1}}_F / \norm{P_{k+1}}_F
\end{equation}
where \( K_k, P_k \) are the solution of the optimization, and
\begin{equation}
	\phi(K_k, P_k) := (A_k + B_k K_k) P_k (A_k + B_k K_k)^\top + G_k G_k^\top.
\end{equation}
A loss larger than 0.1 was observed in all cases.

\section{Conclusions}
We proposed a novel method for solving the chance-constrained covariance steering problem. Due to the nonconvex representation of the square root covariance propagation equations, the problem is solved iteratively via sequential convex programming. Nevertheless, the method is shown to be 1. globally optimal when chance constraints are not present, 2. more scalable for large horizon length than the block approach, and 3. numerically reliable for problems with small variance components compared to the full covariance approach. Furthermore, it is free of any proofs of convexification that rely on a specific objective function form.

A seemingly weak point of the proposed method, requiring an initial guess for the reference variables, can be mitigated especially when it is used repeatedly, for example when the state matrices are updated in a nonlinear dynamics setting \cite{kumagaiRobustCislunarLowThrust2025a}; the initial guess and the Lagrange multipliers of the converged solution can be used to warm-start the next solution process.

The concept of using the QR decomposition and its derivative can be used in other trajectory optimization methods that involve covariance propagation. Furthermore, as in the intrinsic SCvx framework \cite{kraislerIntrinsicSuccessiveConvexification2025a}, the proposed method can be extended to use geodesic distance between matrices in \( \mathbb{L}_+^n \) \cite{linRiemannianGeometrySymmetric2019} to define the trust region and the augmented penalty.
